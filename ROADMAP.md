# Terra Nova roadmap

What's still to build, ordered by impact (re-prioritised 2026-09-28). Pick from the top unless told otherwise. When an item (or one of its slices) lands, delete it here in the same commit.

Rules that apply to every item:
- **Render-only work stays render-only.** Never draw from the game RNG in render code (use `Math.random` or a hash, as `pigs.ts` and `idle.ts` do). The headless baselines must stay bit-identical: `npx tsx scripts/baseline.ts passive 199 1 1`.
- **Anything with a new material** must be compiled in `GameRenderer.warmUp()`, or the first time it appears the game freezes for a frame. A material made per object and disposed when it is done with (sites, scorch marks) takes its shader with it: keep a sample alive (`warmKeep`, or a hidden prototype like the demolition's scorch mark).
- **Finished buildings are drawn in batches** (`src/render/buildingBatches.ts`, three draws a pass: every library material in one shader, by family, `src/render/buildingFamilies.ts`; a building's pieces have their matrices composed once, `freeze` in `buildings.ts`, so whatever moves a building calls `updateMatrix` on it). A batched piece takes its look from its material's row in the families' table (colour, roughness, metalness, emissive, normal scale, snow, grime, texture layer, read by `lookOf` in `materials.ts`) and its textures from the families' texture arrays, whose layers are the pairs in `MAT_TEX_KEYS` (`textures.ts`, each at most 512² and dividing it; at Low, whose textures are made at half size, the layers are 256²): a library material with a new texture pair, or with anything else the table does not carry (another kind of map, transparency, a patch hook of its own), needs it added there and to the family shader, or its batched pieces will not show it (`gr.buildings.setFamilies(false)` draws a batch per material with the library's `batch` twins, for comparisons). While batched, a building's own static meshes are out of the scene graph altogether (`Batched.items[].parent` is where each goes back; hidden meshes would still be walked by every pass and by the matrix update, and a town has thousands). Anything that changes a finished building's meshes or materials must take it out of the batches first, as burning does; moving parts (named movers), flags and sites stay separate meshes.
- **A building has three models** (`ModelBuilder.build` in `src/render/geom.ts`): near, the procedural far one from `FAR_DIST` (36), and from `COARSE_DIST` (64) the far one simplified by meshopt to about a fifth of its triangles within `COARSE_ERR` (12 cm), shared by every player's copy (`coarseOf`). The shadow map draws the coarse model once the view shows it (`withFar(fn, level)`), and always at Low, the reflection always (`showCoarsest`). At Low small things (settlers, animals, birds, lanterns, signs, border posts, fields, vines, goods piles, arrows, debris: `lowShadowSkip` in the renderer) cast no shadow unless the view is closer than `LOW_SMALL_SHADOWS` (20). Trees switch to their far parts once the far error (`TREE_FAR_ERR`, 8 cm) spans under `TreesRenderer.lodPixels` (3) on screen: foliage bears more than the `LOD_PIXELS` (1) the rest of the LODs use. The water keeps each reflection for two frames at every level (four in quiet mode), three times as long (twice in quiet mode) once the view has come to rest with no ship in it, and leaves out settlers, animals, birds, lanterns and border posts.
- **Instanced meshes** finish their writes with `commitInstances` / `uploadFirst` (`uploadChanged` for per-instance data that seldom changes, such as colours). Meshes that call `setColorAt` need `withInstanceColor()` when they're created (`src/render/instancing.ts`). An instanced renderer whose instances have not moved keeps last frame's (`InstanceKeep` in `src/render/lod.ts`, keyed on the view's and the shadow camera's versions and its own data), as the trees and rocks do; the goods piles are laid out again only when the list of piles changes.
- **A material is drawn by one kind of object, and its program key is its code.** three looks a material's program up again at every switch between plain, instanced and batched objects (or with and without per-instance colours): library materials have a twin per kind (`getMaterial(key, kind)` in `src/render/materials.ts`), instanced and batched meshes get a depth material of their own kind (`ownDepth`), and a transparent double-sided material is hidden while it has nothing to draw. `patchMaterial`'s program key is the code it injects (`codeKey`), so materials whose shaders come out the same share one program; an extra `onBeforeCompile` on top of a patch must keep its material's key unique. Draws that cut pixels out (alpha test, `discard`) are ordered after all solid ones and the rest by program (`cutOrder`), which a tile-based GPU needs to skip the pixels covered later. The sky is the last solid draw (`material.userData.drawLate` 2), and on a GPU that shades fragments in the order drawn (Intel, AMD, NVIDIA: `immediateMode` in `hardware.ts`) the ground just before it (`TerrainRenderer.drawLate`, 1, with a strict depth test so a tie still goes to what stands on it), so they are skipped wherever something already covers them; not on Apple's GPUs, where the ground drawn last made a High frame 10% dearer.
- **The view comes to rest, and what stands still is reused.** The camera's eased values land on their goals within a fraction of a pixel (`ease` in `src/render/camera.ts`), so a view left alone is exactly still after about two seconds; the ambient occlusion is then worked out once every `PostFX.aoHold` (4) frames (`aoHalfRate`, keyed on the camera's matrices), and the reflection is kept longer (see above). New work that could be reused between frames should key on the same thing.
- **The shroud is not shaded.** Every opaque patched material writes the shroud's colour and returns at the top of its fragment shader where the exploration is under `uShroudCut` (decided for whole 2x2 pixel blocks, `SHROUD_CUT` in `src/render/shaderPatch.ts`); transparent ones can opt in after their colour code (`PatchOpts.shroudLate`, the water). Code inserted before the end of a patched shader must not contain `#include <opaque_fragment>` (the lamps and the shroud are put in front of its first occurrence).
- **Night lights go through the lamps' grid** (`lightGrid` in `src/render/shaderPatch.ts`): the frame's 32 lamps (`G.uLights`, the nearest the view's target whose reach touches the view) are marked, a bit each, in the cells of a 64x64 grid round the target that their light reaches (`lampReach(w)`, the same reach the shader's attenuation ends at), and a lit pixel works out only the lamps of its cell, in the lamps' own order, so the sum is the one the full loop gave. A new kind of light is a lamp in that list; a change to the attenuation must keep `lampReach` its exact end. (A per-lamp branch in the shader's loop instead made it half as dear again on Apple's GPU.) Every patched program now has a sampler more; WebGL2 guarantees 16 a fragment shader (Chrome on the M5 has exactly 16) and the terrain's uses about 14, so a new texture in `patchMaterial` must be counted against that.
- **The sun's shadow map covers only what the view shows** (`src/render/shadowFit.ts`, checked by `scripts/shadowfit.ts`): the ground in view within 1.1 view sizes of the target, and things up to `TALL` (8) above it. Anything taller that should take a shadow at the edge of the view needs `TALL` raised.
- **Every player action is a command** (`src/game/commands.ts`): the interface never calls into the game or writes it; it issues a command and hears how it went through a `cmd` event (the HUD's `issue()`). A game with a friend replays the same commands on both machines (`src/net/lockstep.ts`), so anything the interface did to the game directly would drift them apart. Game logic reads each player's own fog from `world.seen` (a bit per player); `world.explored` is only this machine's view. `npx tsx scripts/lockstep.ts` fails on a direct call or write from `src/ui`, `src/render` or `src/main.ts`, and plays a jittery three-machine game to catch a drift.
- **The game's arithmetic is the same in every browser**: no `Math.sin/cos/atan2/hypot/pow` and no `**` in `src/game` (the engines round them differently in the last bit); use `sin`, `cos`, `atan2`, `hypot` and `sq` from `src/core/fmath.ts`. Render code may use `Math.*` freely. `scripts/lockstep.ts` checks.
- **Each gameplay feature gets a headless check** in `scripts/`.
- **The tutorial and the campaign are data** (`src/game/missions.ts` and `src/game/regions.ts`, framework `src/game/campaign.ts`, the province's rules `src/game/province.ts`, the designed maps `src/game/recipes.ts`): a mission is a map, cumulative unlocks, rules for its rivals, a setup run once on a fresh game, goals with `done()`, and the quaestor's tips; every goal has a `satisfy()` and every mission a `probe()`, which `npx tsx scripts/campaign.ts` drives (`seeds <id> <from> <to>` finds maps that fit, `time <id>` lets a level-1 AI play it). Everything the game does for a mission is gated on `opts.mission`, so free play and the passive baseline stay bit-identical. The narration (`voice/SCRIPT.md`, `voice/lines.json`) is generated from the missions by `scripts/voicelines.ts`: change the words there, never in the script; the recordings go to `public/voice/<id>.mp3` and `--check` lists the ones still missing.
- **A map is data** (`src/game/map.ts`, the format and the `MapBuilder` API; MAPS.md is the guide): a game started on one (`GameOptions.map`) lays it in deterministically, the save carries it, and `scripts/mapedit.ts` checks that a captured generator's map plays exactly as its seed does, so the generator's order of trees, rocks and deer is part of the format. The editor (`src/ui/editor.ts`) works the world's own arrays through the builder and keeps the world's trees, rocks, deer and headquarters in step after each change; anything new a map holds needs a place in `MapData`, in `applyMap`, in the builder's `cut`/`paste` (undo) and in the editor's sync.
- **Low draws the ground and the stones without their close-up detail layers** (`DETAIL_LOW`, set by `DetailUsers` in `src/render/terrainDetail.ts` from `applyQuality`): the arrays are built only the first time a frame draws them, so a machine that plays at Low never paints or uploads them. A new material that reads `tDetail`/`tDetailN` joins `DetailUsers` and keeps its detail inside `#ifndef DETAIL_LOW`. Low also has no environment map: the sky's light reaches the materials as spherical harmonics (`Sky.setEnvMap`, a `LightProbe` worked out on the CPU from the same sky the map is filtered from, `skySH`), so no material may count on `scene.environment` being there.
- **Frames nobody needs are not drawn** (`src/render/framePace.ts`, checked by `scripts/framepace.ts`): the main loop sets `framePace.rest` before every frame — none at all while the window is not in front (the game goes on unseen), about 10 a second for a game alone under the Esc menu (`IDLE_FPS`; its graphics page keeps 30, to show its settings live), about 30 on the title screen, in the menu otherwise and, once the player has touched nothing for three seconds and the view has come to rest, in a paused game, in the editor and in quiet mode. While the frames rest the interface's own endless animations rest too (`#ui.resting`, set by the loop): a CSS animation that never stops has the browser composite frames at the display's own rate however few the game draws. Quiet mode (`RenderSettings.quiet`, on for machines that run on a battery, `portable()` in `src/render/hardware.ts`) also draws at most 1.25 device pixels per CSS pixel and is fitted to half the frame budget, so a laptop's fans stay off; bloom and the tilt-shift blur are off at every level and the cap defaults to about 60; a machine the ladder takes down to Low aims at 30 frames a second instead (`LOW_FPS`: Low is judged against that budget and the detection sets the cap to match). Anything new that animates on its own should look right at 30 frames a second.
- **A new heavy effect gets its switch in each detection level** (`PRESETS` in `src/render/hardware.ts`, checked by `scripts/hardware.ts`): new players get the level their machine was timed to hold, so an effect that is on everywhere by default lands on machines that were never timed with it.

## 1. Cheaper frames: fewer GL calls, fewer shaded pixels (performance, a quiet machine)

Where a frame's time goes now (3440×1440, a 25-minute town at High with AO, still view, 2026-10-01): the GPU takes about 7.3 ms by day and 8.6 at night at the play zoom (the night lights were 2 ms of it before the lamps' grid), 4.5-5 far out; Chrome's GPU process executes some 1,800-2,500 GL calls a frame (about 1.6 µs each; the reflection pass alone is ~700), the page's main thread about 4.6 ms of JavaScript. Fewer GL calls are the lever for the CPU (and the fans), fewer shaded pixels for the GPU. `PERFPLAN.md` is the research behind this list (79 ideas checked against the code and the measurements, what was done, what was dropped and why, and what to measure first); its first phase is done, and its "Continue here" section holds the user's decisions of 2026-10-01 (FXAA as an option, small shadows by screen size, lower rests, hidden-tab audio, simulation changes that move the baselines, an autosave setting) with the designs to build next.

- **Anti-aliasing**: FXAA (or SMAA) as a mode instead of 4x MSAA on High, the final pass into an LDR target and the AA pass after it: MSAA's measured ceiling is 0.8-0.95 ms, so about 0.3-0.6 ms net, at the price of softer textures and shimmering masts and posts (a player's option). Temporal AA with upscaling comes after it, if at all: most of its gain is fewer pixels, which the resolution setting already offers.
- **The reflection where only the sky can show** (open sea, bare shores) skipped, with the clouds and the moon added to the water's own sky term: 0.4-1 ms on such views; how often they occur is to be measured first.
- **Ambient occlusion** accumulated over frames while the view moves (4 samples instead of 12, 8 denoise taps): 0.35-0.6 ms while moving; at 0.4 of the scene instead of 0.5 in quiet mode.
- **Spherical-harmonics light on rough materials** at High, the environment map kept for the glossy ones: the map's measured ceiling is 0.7 ms at the play zoom, so perhaps 0.4-0.5 ms.
- **The buildings' sub-draws**: a finished building is ~25-30 batch instances (one per material piece), so the families still make 500-900 emulated draws a frame (ANGLE loops a multi-draw into backend draws); one sub-draw per building, level and family at batch-add time would cut that to ~150-250. Measure what one sub-draw costs first (on D3D11 too). Settlers and goods piles must not become BatchedMeshes: three has no instanced multi-draw, so each settler would be ~10 sub-draws.
- **Small casters out of the shadow pass by screen size** at every level, as Low does under 20 units: ~0.4 ms of CPU a frame zoomed out, no GPU change.
- **High-refresh screens**: in a rest the page still takes every animation frame (175 a second to draw 30); asking for the next one with a timer shortly before it is due would save most of them (the pacer must then learn the display's period from `dt / every()`).
- **An opaque canvas** (`alpha: false`, through a context made for three): nothing on macOS, perhaps 0.3-0.5 ms of compositor time a frame on Windows.
- **Indexed building pieces**: ≤1% on the M5, perhaps more on Intel.
- MSAA is a fixed cost of the four-sample HDR target, not its bandwidth (an R11G11B10F target, invalidating its depth or 2x samples saved little or nothing); passes are cheap only on an idle GPU (with other pages on it the bloom's passes took ~0.1 ms each however small).

## 4. Morning mist and sun shafts

Height fog pools in valleys and over water at dawn and burns off by mid-morning, driven by the existing day cycle in `sky.ts`. Add sun shafts as a cheap screen-space pass (a radial blur of what blocks the sun, from depth), folded into the single final pass in `postfx.ts`. There's nothing like either yet. It costs GPU time, so it comes after the performance items.

## 5. Bigger maps

Buildings are batched (`buildingBatches.ts`), so a bigger town adds instances rather than draw calls, and the simulation has plenty of headroom: a 50 ms tick costs 0.05–0.08 ms on average (0.25 ms at the 95th percentile) at 10–45 minutes of AI-vs-AI, so it never needs a Web Worker. Today towns peak around 290 settlers and 90 buildings on the 160 map. Recheck the AI deadlocks listed in the balance notes on larger maps. The worn paths (`src/render/trails.ts`, four texels a node) are tuned on towns of the 160 map (`A0`: about the busiest tenth of the walked-over ground goes bare), so a much busier town wears more of itself bare: look at them again there.

## 9. Maps of the players' own: what's left

The editor, the `MapBuilder` API and map files stand (MAPS.md). Left for later:

- **A map with a friend.** The host's map is not sent through the room (`StartMsg` carries only the options; a map is 50–200 KB of typed arrays, so it wants its own binary action in `room.ts`, chunked by Trystero). Until then the lobby hosts the generator's map.
- **A mission on a map of one's own**: the campaign's missions are data (`Mission.map` is a seed); letting a mission name a map file would let people script scenarios with the campaign's rules (rivals, raids, goals).
- **The editor on touch**: the brushes take a mouse; a finger should paint and two should move the view.
- **Sharing**: a gallery of maps by link (`?map=<url>` plays any fetched map file already), and the map's name and author on the loading screen and in the save list (`SaveMeta.map` holds the name).

## 10. Playing with a friend: what's left

Two people can play over the internet with no server (title screen → Play with a friend: WebRTC between the browsers, found through Trystero over public Nostr relays by a six-letter room code; deterministic lockstep at sixty ticks a second, turns of 100 ms, the input delay set by the host from the measured round trip, a state hash compared every turn). Left for later:

- **Three or four people.** The protocol is symmetric (`scripts/lockstep.ts` already plays three seats); the lobby seats only the first friend, and a leaver's realm needs handing to an AI at a turn everyone agrees on (the lowest remaining seat decides and sends it as a command), where today the game simply stands still.
- **Mending a drift.** A desync freezes the game with a dialog. The host could send a snapshot (`save.ts`) that every machine, host included, restores at the same turn: two restores of one save run identically (`scripts/saveload.ts`), a live game and its restore do not.
- **Saving a game with a friend**, to pick up together later (the autosave and Save page are off in one).
- **A TURN relay** for the pairs of NATs that STUN cannot cross. The NAT lab (`scripts/nettest/`, docker compose: two browsers in two LANs behind iptables routers, a coturn server and the game on a fake internet, the real Nostr relays through the routers; `run.sh [minutes] [scenario...]`) shows what to expect: two cone NATs connect directly (`srflx ↔ srflx`), a symmetric NAT against a port-restricted one does not, nor two symmetric ones, and with a TURN server offered the symmetric pair plays through the relay (about 580 ms to a command's answer at an input delay of 5 turns). The page takes servers from the URL (`?ice=stun:host:3478,turn:user:pass@host:3478`, `&icepolicy=relay`, `&relays=wss://...`) - what is missing is a public TURN server offered by default (a free Open Relay account, or one of ours). Two tabs on one machine only meet on the dev server, where mDNS host candidates are rewritten to loopback (`_test_only_mdnsHostFallbackToLoopback`, localhost only); an embedded browser does not resolve them and one NAT rarely hairpins. `scripts/nettest.mjs` plays two real Chromes on one machine through the real relays (`--drop host` forces the STUN path, `--trace` logs the candidates and pairs).
- **The input delay** is set once from one `ping` in the lobby; a guest whose page is still shaping the title world answers late (the lab saw 1.1-1.6 s where the link had 2-16 ms) and gets the longest delay. Take the least of a few pings, and let the delay follow the connection during play (a word from the host when the game is paused would help too).
- Chat, and names of the players' own choosing (the realms' names stand in).
